### 1. 🚨 [DRAFT — ACTION REQUIRED] RBI Proposes Ban on Revolving Credit for NBFCs

**What:** RBI draft proposes NBFCs can ONLY offer term loans — no more revolving credit (flexi-loans, pay-later, credit lines).

**Issuer:** RBI — Department of Regulation
**Date:** August 6, 2026
**Reference:** Draft "Reserve Bank of India (Non-Banking Financial Companies – Credit Facilities) Amendment Directions, 2026"
**Comment Deadline:** ⚠️ **August 28, 2026** (one week away!)

**In Plain English:**
RBI wants to fundamentally change how NBFCs lend. Under the proposal (new Paragraph 108A):
- NBFCs can **only** offer **term loans** (fixed amount, fixed repayment schedule)
- **Revolving credit is banned** — meaning credit lines, buy-now-pay-later, flexi-EMI facilities where you repay and re-borrow without fresh appraisal
- **Exception:** NBFCs specifically authorised to issue credit cards can still offer revolving credit
- The draft also inserts new definitions of "revolving credit" and "term loan"
- Removes Part D (Demand/Call Loans) from Chapter VIII

**Impact on NBFCs:**
- Massive disruption for fintech-NBFC partnerships offering pay-later, BNPL, credit line products
- Any facility where a customer repays and re-borrows without fresh underwriting would be "revolving"
- Product redesign required — convert credit lines to term loans
- Each draw-down would need fresh appraisal/loan agreement

**Muthoot Fincorp Note:**
- **Low direct impact** on core gold loan business (already term loans / bullet repayment)
- However, if MFL offers any MSME working capital credit lines or flexi-repayment products, those would need review
- Gold loan renewals/top-ups under the new gold loan directions would need fresh appraisal (consistent with this draft)

**FP&A Action Items:**
- **URGENT:** Submit feedback/comments before August 28 if MFL has any revolving credit products
- Review product portfolio for any revolving credit exposure
- Assess impact on any fintech partnerships offering credit-line products

### 2. 🚨 [DRAFT] RBI Draft Interest Rate Directions 2026 — Harmonised Pricing Framework

**What:** RBI proposes unified interest rate regulation for ALL regulated entities (banks, NBFCs, co-ops) replacing six separate circulars.

**Issuer:** RBI — Department of Regulation
**Date:** August 12, 2026
**Reference:** Draft "Reserve Bank of India (Interest Rates on Loans and Advances) Directions, 2026"
**Comment Deadline:** September 11, 2026
**Proposed Effective Date:** April 1, 2027

**In Plain English:**
RBI wants one rulebook for how ALL lenders set interest rates:

| Feature | Requirement |
|---------|-------------|
| **Pricing model** | Benchmark + risk-based spread (similar to MCLR for banks) |
| **Benchmark** | Lender's base reference rate (RBLR for banks) |
| **Spread** | Risk-based — can vary by borrower profile |
| **Floating rate** | Spread **frozen for minimum 3 years** after sanction |
| **Sub-₹50K loans** | Board-approved **APR ceiling** (no numerical cap yet, but must not be "usurious") |
| **Interest calculation** | Daily on unpaid principal, posted monthly |
| **Disclosure** | Full KFS with APR, all fees, spread component |

**Impact on NBFCs:**
- **Spread frozen for 3 years** is a big change — currently NBFCs can revise spreads
- APR ceiling for microfinance and small personal loans (up to ₹50,000) — RBI hasn't proposed a number but requires boards to set one
- More transparency required — breakdown of benchmark vs spread in disclosures
- Aligns NBFC pricing framework with banks for the first time

**Muthoot Fincorp Note:**

- MFI portfolio (via Muthoot Microfin) would be directly impacted by the APR ceiling requirement
- Board resolution needed to set APR ceiling for loans ≤₹50,000
- FP&A impact: pricing model overhaul, system changes for benchmark-linked pricing
- **Important:** This is a DRAFT — nothing changes until April 2027 if finalized

**FP&A Action Items:**

- Model impact of spread freeze on NIM

# Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) for NBFCs

**What is it?**
The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 is India's unified law for resolving insolvency of companies, partnerships, and individuals. For NBFCs, it provides both a mechanism for recovery when borrowers default AND a framework for resolution if the NBFC itself becomes insolvent. Think of it as the "last resort" — when normal debt recovery fails, IBC steps in.

---

**The Technical Details**

### I. IBC as a Recovery Tool FOR NBFCs (Creditors)

When an NBFC's borrower defaults, the NBFC can approach the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) under IBC:

**Section 7 — Application by Financial Creditor**
| Element | Requirement |
|---------|-------------|
| **Who can file** | Any financial creditor (including NBFCs) |
| **Minimum default** | **₹1 crore** (threshold for individual applications) |
| **What to prove** | (a) Default exists, (b) Debt is due, (c) Event of default has occurred |
| **Application fee** | ₹25,000 |
| **Tribunal** | NCLT (National Company Law Tribunal) |

**Key point:** For NBFCs, the ₹1 crore threshold applies to individual filings. However, RBI has special powers under Section 70A (see below) with a ₹1,000 crore threshold.

### Section 70A — RBI's Special Power for Large NBFCs

This is a critical provision unique to NBFCs:

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **Power** | RBI can **direct** NCLT to initiate CIRP against an NBFC |
| **Threshold** | NBFC with **₹1,000+ crore** in debt |
| **Rationale** | Large NBFC failures can cause systemic risk |
| **RBI's role** | Files application as "financial creditor" |
| **Trigger** | RBI determines resolution is in public interest |

**This is India's equivalent of the UK's Bank of England resolution powers.** RBI has used this power — notably in the case of DHFL (2021), which was the first NBFC referred under Section 70A.

### II. Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP)

Once NCLT admits the application, the following process unfolds:

| Stage | Timeline | Key Actions |
|-------|----------|-------------|
| **Moratorium** | Day 1 onwards | All proceedings against the company stay — no lawsuits, no asset seizure, no transfers |
| **IRP Appointment** | Within 14 days | Interim Resolution Professional takes charge |
| **CoC Formation** | Within 30 days | Committee of Creditors (all financial creditors, proportional to exposure) |
| **Resolution Plan** | 180 days (extendable to 270, max 330) | Resolution Applicant submits plan; CoC approves with 66% vote |
| **Liquidation** | If no plan accepted | Company wound up; assets distributed per waterfall |

**Critical concept — Moratorium:**
Once IBC proceedings start, there is an **automatic stay** on:
- No debt recovery proceedings
- No asset transfer or sale
- No court/tribunal proceedings
- No attachment or encumbrance

**For NBFCs (gold loan context):** This means if a gold loan borrower's assets are under moratorium, the NBFC **cannot auction the gold** during the CIRP period. The gold remains with the NBFC as custodian, but can't be disposed.

### III. Priority of Claims (Waterfall Distribution)

If resolution fails and the company goes into liquidation, assets are distributed in this order:

| Priority | Claim | Period |
|----------|-------|--------|
| 1 | Insolvency Resolution Process Costs | — |
| 2 | Workmen's dues | 24 months preceding liquidation |
| 3 | Employee dues (other than workmen) | 12 months preceding |
| 4 | **Financial creditors (secured)** | — |
| 5 | Government dues | 12 months preceding |
| 6 | **Financial creditors (unsecured)** | — |
| 7 | Trade/operational creditors | — |
| 8 | Remaining government dues | — |
| 9 | Equity shareholders | — |

**For NBFCs:** As a secured financial creditor, MFL would rank at **Priority 4** in the waterfall. This is higher than most operational creditors and government dues (above ₹12 months).

### IV. Pre-Packaged Insolvency (PIRP) for MSMEs

IBC provides a simplified process for MSMEs:

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Eligible** | MSMEs (under MSMED Act) |
| **Default limit** | Up to **₹1 crore** |
| **Initiated by** | Debtor (not creditor) |
| **Process** | Negotiated resolution with existing management |
| **Timeline** | 120 days |
| **Cost** | Lower than full CIRP |
| **Approval** | 66% of financial creditors |

**Muthoot relevance:** If MFL has MSME borrowers who default, PIRP could be an alternative to standard recovery. However, ₹1 crore limit is quite low for most MSME loans.

### V. Personal Guarantors

IBC can initiate proceedings against **personal guarantors** to corporate debtors:

| Aspect | Rule |
|--------|------|
| **Who** | Individuals who guaranteed corporate debt |
| **Threshold** | ₹1,000 crore (for RBI-initiated action) |
| **Personal assets** | Can be attached |
| **Bankruptcy** | Individual can be declared bankrupt |
| **Moratorium** | Applies to guarantor's personal assets too |

**For gold loan NBFCs:** If a gold loan borrower is also a personal guarantor to a large corporate default, their personal assets (including pledged gold) could be affected by IBC proceedings against the corporate debtor.

---

**Why It Matters for NBFCs**

IBC fundamentally changed debt recovery in India:

1. **Recovery rates improved:** Pre-IBC, recovery rates were ~20-25%. Post-IBC, they've improved to ~35-40% (higher for secured creditors)

2. **Time-bound resolution:** Before IBC, debt recovery through courts took 4-5 years. IBC mandates 180+90 = 270 days (with extensions up to 330 days)

3. **Moratorium risk:** For gold-loan NBFCs, the biggest risk is that a defaulting borrower enters CIRP and the moratorium prevents gold auction. The gold sits with the NBFC but can't be monetized until resolution.

4. **Systemic risk management:** RBI's Section 70A power is specifically designed for large NBFCs — this creates regulatory oversight for "too-big-to-fail" NBFCs.

**Quiz Yourself:**

1. **Question:** A gold-loan NBFC has lent ₹3 crore to a company that has now defaulted. Can the NBFC file an IBC application directly, or does it need RBI approval?
   **Answer:** The NBFC can file directly under Section 7 (threshold ₹1 crore). No RBI approval needed. RBI's Section 70A power (₹1,000 crore threshold) is separate — used only for large systemic-risk cases where RBI itself files.

2. **Question:** A borrower's company enters CIRP on January 1. The gold-loan NBFC holds ₹50 lakh worth of pledged gold. Can the NBFC auction this gold in February?
   **Answer:** No. The moratorium under Section 14 prevents any disposal of assets, including auction of pledged gold. The gold remains with the NBFC as custodian. Auction can only happen after resolution plan is approved or liquidation order is passed.

3. **Question:** What happens to an unsecured operational creditor (say, a supplier) vs MFL (secured financial creditor) in liquidation?
   **Answer:** MFL ranks at Priority 4 (secured financial creditor). The supplier ranks at Priority 7 (trade/operational creditor). MFL gets paid first from the waterfall. This is why secured lending (like gold loans) has a structural advantage in recovery.
