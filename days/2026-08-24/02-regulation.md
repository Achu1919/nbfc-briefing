### Update 1: RBI Draft Circular — Ban on Revolving Credit for NBFCs

- **What:** RBI proposes to restrict all NBFCs (except those authorised to issue credit cards) to offering only term loans — banning revolving credit products entirely.
- **Issuer:** RBI (Department of Regulation)
- **Date:** Draft published August 6, 2026 | Feedback deadline: **August 28, 2026**
- **Reference:** Draft RBI (Non-Banking Financial Companies – Credit Facilities) Amendment Directions, 2026
- **Classification:** [NEW_REG] — Draft, under public consultation

**In Plain English:** RBI wants NBFCs to stop offering products where money flows back and forth (like overdrafts, flexi-loans, credit-on-UPI). From now on, NBFCs can only give fixed-term loans with a defined repayment schedule. The only exception: NBFCs already authorised to issue credit cards (like SBI Cards). NBFCs wanting to issue credit cards independently need ₹100 crore NOF and prior RBI approval.

**Impact on NBFCs:**
- Bajaj Finance, Shriram Finance, Tata Capital, and other large NBFCs with revolving credit books are affected. Industry estimates ₹2+ lakh crore in credit products could be disrupted.
- Products like loans against shares, flexi personal loans, supply-chain finance, and credit-on-UPI could be reclassified.
- If finalised as-is, NBFCs would face regulatory arbitrage vs banks (banks can still offer working capital/overdraft facilities).

**RBI DG Clarification (August 19):** Deputy Governor Shirish Chandra Murmu told CNBC-TV18 the draft was intended to "bring greater clarity" to existing rules, not to prevent business activity. "Our regulation will not come in the way of any business activity." This suggests the final version may be narrower than the draft.

**Muthoot Fincorp Note:** MFL primarily offers gold loans (term loans by nature) and microfinance (also term-based). **Low direct impact** on MFL's core products. However, if MFL offers any flexible/revolving credit products for MSME clients, those would need review. Monitor for any MFL group entities that might offer revolving facilities.

### Update 3: RBI Draft — Comprehensive Interest Rate Framework for All Lenders

- **What:** RBI proposes harmonised interest rate directions covering all regulated entities (banks, NBFCs, HFCs, AIFIs, co-op banks) — for the first time bringing NBFCs under formal interest rate regulation.
- **Issuer:** RBI (Department of Regulation)
- **Date:** Draft published August 12, 2026 | Feedback deadline: **September 11, 2026**
- **Reference:** Draft RBI (Interest Rates on Loans and Advances) Directions, 2026 | Press Release 2026-2027/877
- **Classification:** [NEW_REG] — Draft, under public consultation

**Key Provisions:**

| Provision | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Effective date** | April 1, 2027 for new loans; April 1, 2029 for migration of existing loans |
| **Monthly rests** | Interest must be charged at monthly rests (currently only required for banks) |
| **Daily reducing balance** | Interest must be computed on daily reducing balance basis — no flat-rate lending |
| **APR ceiling** | NBFCs must set internal APR ceiling for microfinance loans and small-value loans (principal ≤ ₹50,000) — ensuring they are "not usurious" |
| **Benchmark methodology** | Must be publicly disclosed; spread components (CRP, operating cost, term premium, business strategy premium) must be defined |
| **Reset frequency** | Floating rate benchmarks must be reset at least every 3 months |
| **Spread freeze** | Spread cannot be revised for 3 years on floating-rate loans (exception: customer retention with justification) |
| **No lending below benchmark** | Loan cannot be priced below the applicable benchmark (may impact interest subvention schemes) |
| **Transition** | Existing loans migrated by April 1, 2029, with borrower consent, no disadvantage, no charges |

**Impact on NBFCs:**
- **First-ever formal interest rate regulation for NBFCs.** Historically, NBFCs only had conduct-related rules, not pricing rules.
- Flat-rate lending (common in microfinance and small-ticket loans) will end — must compute on daily reducing balance.
- Internal APR caps for small loans will bring regulatory oversight to rates that were previously unbridled (some small-ticket lenders charge 50–60%+ APR).
- Gold loan interest calculation methodology may need review if not already on daily reducing balance.
- Spread freeze for 3 years limits repricing flexibility.

**Muthoot Fincorp Note:** This is **material for MFL** — affects multiple product lines:
- **Gold loans:** MFL should verify gold loan interest computation is on daily reducing balance. Bullet repayment structure needs review under "monthly rests" requirement (interest accrues monthly even if payment is at maturity).
- **Microfinance:** Must set internal APR ceiling. Current MFI rates need to be benchmarked against the "not usurious" standard.
- **MSME lending:** Spread freeze for 3 years affects repricing of floating-rate MSME loans.
- **Action required:** Board-level review of interest rate policy by April 2027. Existing loan migration planning by April 2029.

- **What:** New framework for appointment/reappointment of Statutory Central Auditors and Statutory Auditors for all NBFCs including HFCs.
- **Issuer:** RBI (Department of Supervision)
- **Date:** July 31, 2026 (immediate effect)
- **Reference:** RBI/DoS/2026-27/465
- **Classification:** [CIRCULAR] — Operational instruction

**Key Provisions:**
- **Joint audit mandatory** for NBFCs with asset size ≥ ₹15,000 crore (minimum 2 audit firms, no common partners).
- **3-year continuous tenure** for auditors, followed by **6-year cooling-off** before reappointment in the same NBFC.
- **Eligibility norms** based on: partner strength, FCA partners, CISA/ISA qualification, audit experience, professional staff — all tiered by NBFC asset size.
- Non-deposit taking NBFCs with asset size below ₹1,000 crore may continue with existing procedure.
- Audit firm must submit eligibility certificate (Form B) to RBI.

### CRAR Deep Dive — Capital to Risk-Weighted Assets Ratio

**What is it?**

CRAR (Capital to Risk-Weighted Assets Ratio) is the single most important regulatory metric for any financial institution. It measures whether an NBFC has enough capital (its own money, not borrowed) to absorb potential losses from its lending activities. Think of it as a **safety buffer** — the higher the CRAR, the more resilient the NBFC is to unexpected loan defaults.

---

**The Technical Details**

**Formula:**
```
CRAR = (Tier I Capital + Tier II Capital) / Risk-Weighted Assets × 100
```

**Tier I Capital (Going-Concern Capital):**
This is the "first loss" absorbent — capital that can absorb losses while the NBFC continues operating.
- Paid-up equity share capital
- Share premium
- Free reserves (including quarterly profits, subject to dividend adjustment)
- Balance in share forfeited account
- Innovative perpetual debt instruments (for UL NBFCs)
- **Less:** Accumulated losses, intangible assets, deferred revenue expenditure, preliminary expenses

**Tier II Capital (Gone-Concern Capital):**
This absorbs losses after Tier I is depleted — typically when an NBFC is being wound down.
- Revaluation reserves (at 55% discount)
- Outstanding perpetual debt instruments
- Term subordinated debt (minimum 5-year maturity)
- **Less:** Known future claims, revaluation losses

**Risk-Weighted Assets (RWA):**
Not all assets carry the same risk. RBI assigns "risk weights" based on the type of asset:
| Asset Type | Risk Weight |
|------------|-------------|
| Cash, government securities, gold | 0% |
| Loans fully secured by government guarantee | 0% |
| Loans to banks, MFIs | 20% |
| Housing loans (LTV ≤ 80%) | 35%–50% |
| SME loans | 75%–100% |
| Personal loans, unsecured consumer credit | 125% |
| Standard NBFC assets (secured) | 100% |
| Standard NBFC assets (unsecured) | 150% |
| Sub-standard assets | 150% |
| Doubtful assets | 100%–150% |
| Loss assets | 150% |

**Key:** Higher-risk assets consume more capital. An NBFC with a large unsecured loan book needs more capital than one with primarily secured lending (like gold loans).

---

**NBFC CRAR Requirements by SBR Layer:**

| Parameter | NBFC-BL | NBFC-ML | NBFC-UL |
|-----------|---------|---------|---------|
| **Minimum CRAR** | Not prescribed (leverage ratio applies) | **15%** | **15%** (with enhanced buffers) |
| **Minimum Tier I** | Not prescribed | **10%** | **10%** |
| **Tier I for Gold-Loan NBFCs** | N/A | **12%** | **12%** |
| **Capital Conservation Buffer (CCB)** | N/A | 0.625% | 0.625% |
| **Countercyclical Buffer** | N/A | 0–2.5% | 0–2.5% |
| **Leverage Ratio** | Max 7x | Not prescribed | Not prescribed |

**Important:** The **15% CRAR** for NBFC-ML and NBFC-UL is significantly higher than the **9% CRAR** required for banks under Basel III. This reflects RBI's view that NBFCs, lacking deposit insurance and access to RBI liquidity facilities, need a thicker capital cushion.

---

**How CRAR Is Monitored:**

1. **Quarterly reporting:** NBFCs must compute and report CRAR quarterly (ML and UL layers).
2. **CRAR return:** Filed with RBI in prescribed format — includes RWA computation, Tier I/II details.
3. **Supervisory triggers:**
   - CRAR falling below 15%: RBI can impose restrictions (限制 expansion, restrict dividend, require capital plan).
   - CRAR falling below 10%: RBI can supersede board, impose moratorium, or direct merger.
   - CRAR falling below 6%: Potential cancellation of Certificate of Registration.

---

**Recent Changes:**

**March 10, 2026 — RBI Clarification on Tier I Capital Computation:**
- RBI issued Second Amendment Directions on Capital Adequacy (RBI/2025-26/226) clarifying how **quarterly profits** can be included in Tier I.
- Key change: Quarterly profits can now be reckoned for Tier I provided:
  - Financial statements are subjected to limited review/audit by statutory auditors quarterly.
  - Profits are adjusted: `Eligible Profit = Net Profit − (0.25 × Average Dividend of Last 3 Years)`.
  - Losses in the current year must be fully deducted from Owned Fund.

**March 10, 2026 — Concentration Norms Tier I Clarification:**
- For credit/investment concentration norms, Tier I capital must be based on **latest available financial statements** (audited or limited review), not necessarily year-end audited numbers.
- External auditor's certificate required for capital augmentation before reckoning additions.

**Muthoot Fincorp Note:** The quarterly profit adjustment formula is critical for FP&A:
- If MFL pays consistent dividends, a significant portion of quarterly profits will be deducted (25% of 3-year average dividend).
- In loss-making quarters, full deduction from Owned Fund — this can quickly erode CRAR.
- FP&A should model quarterly CRAR projections incorporating this formula.

---

**Why It Matters**

CRAR directly determines:
1. **Growth capacity:** An NBFC cannot grow its loan book if it doesn't have enough capital. Every ₹1 of lending consumes a certain amount of capital based on the risk weight.
2. **Regulatory standing:** Falling below CRAR thresholds triggers supervisory action.
3. **Funding access:** Investors and lenders monitor CRAR closely — a low CRAR makes it harder and more expensive to raise funds.
4. **Dividend policy:** RBI can restrict dividend payments if CRAR is weak.
5. **Strategic options:** M&A, new business lines, geographic expansion — all require capital headroom.

---

- The new quarterly profit adjustment rule means MFL's FP&A team must model CRAR quarterly, not just at year-end.

**Quiz Yourself:**

1. **Q:** If MFL has Tier I capital of ₹1,200 crore and Risk-Weighted Assets of ₹8,000 crore, what is its Tier I ratio? Does it meet the requirement?
   **A:** Tier I ratio = 1,200/8,000 × 100 = 15%. Yes, this meets the 12% Tier I requirement for gold-loan NBFCs. (But note: the overall CRAR must also be ≥ 15%, which requires Tier I + Tier II combined.)

2. **Q:** Why does RBI require a higher Tier I ratio for gold-loan NBFCs (12%) compared to the standard 10%?
   **A:** Gold-loan NBFCs are heavily concentrated in a single asset class (gold collateral). While gold loans are generally low-NPA, the concentration risk means any adverse gold price movement or valuation issues could cause larger-than-expected losses. The higher Tier I requirement acts as an additional buffer.

3. **Q:** Under the new March 2026 amendment, if MFL reports ₹50 crore net profit in Q2 but has paid an average of ₹80 crore annual dividend over the last 3 years, how much of the Q2 profit can be included in Tier I?
   **A:** Eligible Profit = ₹50 cr − (0.25 × ₹80 cr) = ₹50 cr − ₹20 cr = ₹30 crore. Only ₹30 crore of the ₹50 crore quarterly profit can be reckoned for Tier I capital computation.
