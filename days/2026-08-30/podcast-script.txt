It's Sunday, the thirtieth of August, and here's your weekend regulatory briefing.

So the big story from this past week — the comment period on the RBI's revolving credit ban just closed on Friday. August twenty eighth was the last day for industry feedback on the draft amendment to the NBFC Credit Facilities Directions. This is the one where the RBI proposed that NBFCs can only offer term loans, period. No more revolving credit facilities. No credit lines, no top-up mechanisms, nothing that replenishes when you pay it back. If you're not an authorised credit card issuer, you're done with revolving products.

Now, the industry response is going to be interesting because this isn't a fringe product. A lot of NBFCs — especially the smaller ones and fintech-backed lenders — have built their entire working capital and personal loan businesses around credit lines that replenish. The RBI's definition is deliberately broad. A term loan has to be a fixed principal amount, disbursed in instalments, repaid on a predetermined schedule. Once you pay it back, the limit doesn't come back. That's the test. Anything that doesn't meet that definition is, by default, revolving credit. And the draft says NBFCs simply cannot offer it.

For Muthoot Fincorp, this is mostly a non-event in terms of product impact. Gold loans are already term loans with bullet repayment. Personal loans, MSME loans, microfinance — these are all structured as term loans. MFL doesn't really have a credit line business that would get caught by this. But the direction matters. The RBI is signalling very clearly that it wants NBFCs to stay in the lane of straightforward lending. No quasi-banking products. No deposit-taking behaviour without a deposit-taking licence. The question now is what happens after the comment period closes. Whether the RBI will water it down, add carve-outs, or push ahead as is. We should get a sense of that in the next few weeks.

The second story — and this one hits closer to home for MFL — is the draft interest rate directions that the RBI released on August twelfth. Comments on that are still open until September eleventh. This is the big harmonised framework. For the first time, NBFCs are being brought under a formal interest rate regulation regime, the same way banks have been for years.

Here's what it means practically. Every loan an NBFC writes from April first, twenty twenty seven onwards has to be priced off a benchmark, internal or external, plus a spread. You can't just pick a number out of the air. The benchmark has to be published, the spread components have to be documented in your board policy, and you have to disclose the methodology publicly. For floating rate loans, the benchmark resets at least every three months. And the non-credit-risk components of your spread — the operating cost, the term premium, the business strategy piece — those can't be revised for three years on a floating rate loan. The credit risk premium can change, but only when the borrower's actual credit profile changes.

The piece that really matters for MFL is the APR cap on microfinance loans and small value loans. Small value means personal loans where the principal doesn't exceed fifty thousand rupees. The RBI is saying you have to set an explicit ceiling on the all-in cost — interest plus every fee — and it can't be usurious. That's going to force a rethink on how a lot of these products are priced, especially the ones where processing fees, insurance charges, and service fees stack up on top of the headline rate. The direction is clear: the RBI wants transparency in pricing, and it wants a ceiling that protects borrowers.

Existing loans have to be migrated to the new framework by April first, twenty twenty nine. The migration can't disadvantage the borrower — the rate can't go up, and you can't charge a fee for the switch. So this is a two-year runway, but the systems work starts now.

One more thing worth noting. The RBI's concentration risk fourth amendment came through on August twenty fifth. This one's niche — it extends the large exposure limits that apply to NBFC infrastructure finance companies to IDF-NBFCs in the Upper Layer. MFL is not in the Upper Layer, so this doesn't touch you. But it's worth watching because it shows the RBI is still fine-tuning the SBR framework even after the big June overhaul. Small adjustments, targeted at specific entity types. The pattern is the RBI tightening screws gradually, not in one dramatic move.

That's the regulatory week. The revolving credit consultation is done, the interest rate framework consultation is open for another week and a half, and the gold loan rules that kicked in from April are still settling in. For your FP&A planning, the interest rate directions are the one to watch closely. Start mapping your portfolio to the benchmark-plus-spread model now, because when April twenty twenty seven arrives, the systems have to be ready.
